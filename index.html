Saep /sɛ̂p/ — Isan. The specific pleasure of food that hurts a little.

There is no Thai dish that tastes of one thing.

Every plate that leaves this kitchen is an argument between five tastes, settled at the last second, by hand, by tongue. Nothing is measured. Everything is tasted. Scroll down and you go through them one at a time — the way a cook builds a bowl, from the first strike of chilli to the smell that reaches the door.

Taste 01 — the strike

เผ็ด

phet

Spicy

Prik kee noo. Bird's eye, two centimetres, pounded — never chopped. A knife bruises the seed and turns heat into bitterness. In the mortar it opens clean, and it arrives before anything else: a bright flash across the front of the tongue that makes everything after it taste louder.

  • Ratchaburi grower
  • 50–100k Scoville
  • Pounded, not cut

Taste 02 — the inhale

เปรี้ยว

priao

Sour

Lime goes in off the heat. Boil it and it turns metallic and flat. So the pot leaves the fire, the juice goes in last, and the sour lands high and sharp — the note in tom yam that makes you breathe in through your teeth. Tamarind does the same job lower down, slower, for the curries that need weight.

  • Makrut leaf
  • Lime off-heat, always last
  • Tamarind for depth

Taste 03 — the floor

เค็ม

khem

Salty

Not salt. Nam pla. Anchovies and sea salt, a year in the dark, pressed once. Salt is a single note; fermented fish is a chord — savoury, funky, faintly sweet, and it sits underneath the dish holding everything else up. Our som tam gets pla ra as well, which is the part most kitchens outside Thailand quietly leave out.

  • Nam pla, first press
  • 12 months
  • Pla ra in the som tam

Taste 04 — the peacemaker

หวาน

waan

Sweet

Palm sugar, shaved off the disc. Smoky, caramel, slightly grainy — nothing like white sugar, which only makes food sweet. This doesn't sweeten the dish. It rounds the corners, so the chilli and the lime stop shouting at each other and start agreeing. Two shavings too many and the whole bowl goes childish.

  • Nam tan puek
  • Shaved, never measured
  • Coconut sugar for the south

Taste 05 — not a taste at all

หอม

hom

Aromatic

Thai cooks count five, and the fifth is a smell. Lemongrass bruised with the flat of a knife, galangal in coins, makrut torn by hand rather than sliced. They go into the pot whole and you are not meant to eat them — you are meant to smell them from the door, before you have taken your coat off, before you have decided what to order.

  • Takhrai · bruised
  • Kha · in coins
  • Bai makrut · torn

All five, at once

สมดุล

No taste is allowed to win. The cook keeps adjusting until nothing stands out — and everything is loud.

The bowl you have just been walking through

ต้มยำกุ้ง

Tom yam kung · 165 dkk

Wok hei · breath of the wok

20°C

Your hob at home tops out around 180. This one runs at 220 with a forced-air ring that sounds like a runway. Above roughly 200, oil, sugar and protein stop cooking and start reacting: you get char that tastes of smoke without any smoke in it. Twelve seconds too long and it tastes of ash instead. That is the whole job.

The kitchen

Chef Napat “Nok” Thongchai cooked eleven years in Bangkok hotel kitchens making Thai food for people who did not want it to taste Thai. She left in 2016, spent two winters in Copenhagen learning which Nordic ingredients could take the punishment, and opened Saep in a former bicycle workshop on Jægersborggade in 2019.

The rule here has not changed since: nothing is toned down unless you ask, and if you ask, we will tell you what you are giving up.

The sourcing

Fish and shellfish from Danish boats. Duck from Fyn. Holy basil, Thai aubergine and makrut from a grower under glass outside Køge who used to farm tomatoes and now grows almost nothing else. Everything that cannot survive the journey — pla ra, palm sugar, dried chilli — comes in by the case from the same three suppliers we have used since the first year.

Pastes are pounded every morning between eight and ten. You can hear it from the street, which is the only advertising we have ever paid for.
